# The neurology connection

Two failures that define modern neurology

Neurology's two great unsolved problems are really one problem seen from two directions. Neurodegeneration is the slow loss of neurons that were built to last a lifetime and are not replaced: dopaminergic neurons in Parkinson disease, motor neurons in ALS, cortical and hippocampal neurons in Alzheimer disease and the frontotemporal dementias. Failed regeneration is the mirror image: after stroke, trauma, or degeneration, the adult human nervous system does not rebuild, because adult human neurons are post-mitotic, their progenitors are nearly absent, and the maintenance systems that keep them alive degrade with age. Both failures are failures of cellular maintenance over long timescales. Both are exactly the class of problem that a repeatedly rejuvenating nervous system does not have.

The cnidarian nervous system is ancient, real, and rebuildable

It is tempting to dismiss the jellyfish nervous system as too simple to matter for human neurology. That would be a mistake, for three reasons. First, it is genuinely neural: cnidarian neurons use the same neurotransmitter families, the same electrical excitability, and homologs of the same developmental transcription factors as bilaterian neurons, and the cnidarian-bilaterian common ancestor already possessed a differentiated nervous system, making neural cell types among the oldest cell types in animals. Comparative work on cnidarian neurogenesis has been explicit that these animals preserve, in readable form, ancestral features of how nervous systems are made. Second, it is continuously rebuilt. In Hydra, the nerve net is perpetually renewed from interstitial stem cells across the animal's non-aging life. In T. dohrnii, the entire neural architecture of the medusa is dismantled and re-specified during life cycle reversal. Third, it is experimentally tractable: cnidarian genomes are small, their cell lineages are accessible, and gene editing now works in several species.

The relevance to human neurology is not that a jellyfish has a brain. It is that the jellyfish genome encodes the ancestral version of the maintenance and plasticity machinery that human neurons depend on and cannot renew. Where that machinery exists in both species, the jellyfish offers a context in which the machinery runs in a permanently regenerative mode. Where the human disease genes have no jellyfish counterpart, the comparison marks the parts of human neural vulnerability that are evolutionary novelties, layered on top of the ancient core. Panel C: the twenty-four genes and what each costs when it fails

The neurology panel is built from the genes whose failure causes or strongly predisposes to the major human neurodegenerative diseases, plus the neurotrophic signalling system. The Alzheimer module contains APP, the amyloid precursor protein whose cleavage products drive the defining pathology; PSEN1 and PSEN2, the presenilins that form the catalytic core of the gamma-secretase that cuts APP and whose mutations cause aggressive early-onset familial disease; MAPT, encoding tau, whose aggregation defines a family of tauopathies; TREM2, the microglial receptor that links neurodegeneration to the brain's immune compartment; and APOE, the shared bridge with Panel B. The Parkinson module contains SNCA, encoding alpha-synuclein, the aggregating protein of Lewy bodies; LRRK2, the kinase whose hyperactive variants are the most common genetic cause of Parkinson disease; PARK7, encoding DJ-1, an oxidative-stress sensor; PINK1, the mitophagy-initiating kinase; and GBA, the lysosomal glucocerebrosidase whose carrier status is among the strongest known Parkinson risk factors.

The motor-neuron and frontotemporal module contains SOD1, the first discovered ALS gene; TARDBP, encoding TDP-43, whose mislocalisation and aggregation is the pathological signature of most ALS and much frontotemporal dementia; FUS, another aggregating RNA-binding protein; C9orf72, whose hexanucleotide repeat expansion is the most common inherited cause of both ALS and frontotemporal dementia; GRN, encoding progranulin; OPTN, encoding optineurin, an autophagy receptor mutated in ALS and glaucoma; SQSTM1, encoding the autophagy adaptor p62; VCP, the AAA-ATPase at the centre of multisystem proteinopathy; and UBQLN2, a ubiquitin-shuttling quality-control factor mutated in ALS with dementia. The repeat and prion module contains HTT, whose polyglutamine expansion causes Huntington disease, and PRNP, the prion protein. Finally, the trophic module contains BDNF and NGF, the neurotrophins whose signalling sustains neuronal survival and plasticity and whose decline tracks cognitive aging.

Read against the panel, a pattern is already visible before any jellyfish data are considered: most of these genes are not neural-specific. They are maintenance genes, handling protein quality control, lysosomal clearance, mitochondrial surveillance, stress response, and membrane trafficking, that happen to fail first in neurons because neurons are long-lived, post-mitotic, and unable to dilute damage by dividing. This is the deep reason a rejuvenating animal is relevant to neurodegeneration: neurodegeneration is, mechanistically, accelerated local aging of the least replaceable cells. The same hallmarks that organise aging research organise the molecular pathology of Alzheimer, Parkinson, and ALS, as the converging-pathways literature in neurodegeneration has argued for a decade.

The specific questions the jellyfish map can answer

For neurology, the expansion asks three questions of Panel C. Which of these human disease genes have mappable counterparts in the T. dohrnii genome at all? Of those, which are shared with the mortal cnidarians, and which, if any, show copy-number or sequence divergence in the immortal species? And does the set of neurodegeneration-relevant genes present in the jellyfish overlap the longevity set more than chance, which would be genomic evidence for the maintenance-machinery bridge between the two fields? The first question produces an evolutionary annotation of the human disease-gene list: which vulnerabilities are ancient and which are new. The second nominates candidate rejuvenation-associated divergence in neural-relevant genes. The third is the pre-registered H5 test, and its answer, positive or negative, disciplines how strongly the aging-neurology connection can be stated from this dataset.

What this does not claim

It is important to be blunt about the limits. T. dohrnii does not get Alzheimer disease. It has no amyloid cascade to model, no dopaminergic system to lose, no prion to propagate. The presence of a mappable homolog of APP or SNCA in a cnidarian genome says something about the evolutionary age of the protein's normal function, and nothing direct about the human disease, which involves derived features of the human protein, human brain architecture, and human lifespan. The value of the map is upstream of disease modelling: it tells researchers which disease genes sit on ancient, jellyfish-shared foundations, where those foundations might be interrogated in a regenerative whole-animal context, and which disease processes are human- or vertebrate-specific and must be studied in vertebrate systems. A map that knows its own limits is more useful than a model that pretends to be a brain.
