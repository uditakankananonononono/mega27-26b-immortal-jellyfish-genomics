# The annotation desert, revisited

The closed-phase audit measured a narrower, database-specific gap: its organism-and-gene queries to selected NCBI gene, nucleotide and protein fields, with a UniProt check, found no named records for the repair and telomerase panel. That does not mean T. dohrnii has no deposited annotations. The Kyushu genome provider published 23,314 high-confidence predicted genes, their peptides/GFF, and an RNA-based classification; its 2023 paper also used OrthoFinder across cnidarians. The closed-phase search failed to find those resources, so claims that nobody had predicted or deposited genes were wrong. The direct-assembly ledger is still useful as a separate, threshold-frozen homology search, but cannot be portrayed as first gene annotation or as defeating every existing gene-model workflow (Hasegawa et al. 2023, https://academic.oup.com/dnaresearch/article/30/1/dsac047/6909006).

The expansion's targeted NCBI gene/protein query audit likewise returned no entries for its 47 tested gene labels. That result describes the exact query and database fields, not all public gene resources or biological absence. It should motivate cross-checks with the provider's annotations, not a declaration that a genus is unannotated. A predicted gene is also not automatically the sought functional ortholog; both the prediction and our BLAST locus need independent biological validation.

Why the NCBI search did not retrieve the provider's models is not established by this audit. Database indexing, accession choice, naming conventions and query syntax are plausible causes, but these were not systematically isolated. The observed fact is the narrower search miss alongside an existing provider-published gene catalog.

The consequence for aging and neurology research is the part this project cares most about. Negligibly senescent and rejuvenating species are disproportionately non-model: they are interesting precisely because they are not the standard laboratory animals, which means they are precisely the organisms the curated databases serve worst. Comparative longevity research often needs checks across raw assemblies, provider models and indexed databases; our direct mapping was one such check: genome-first, calibration-first, threshold-frozen, controls-always. The pipeline the closed phase built and this expansion reuses is one such method, and it is deliberately mundane: BLAST, a fixed bit-score threshold, locus clustering, controls. Its simplicity helps auditability, but does not establish accuracy without independent checks.

There is also a quiet opportunity in the desert. Even when general search returns nothing, a mapped panel locus is a checkable coordinate hypothesis: a specific locus, on a specific contig, with specific coordinates, identity, coverage, and evidence, recorded in a public ledger. Aggregated across the panels, the ledgers form an independent coordinate index for a selected set of maintenance genes, not a first gene annotation. These ledgers can be compared to the provider's existing annotations. The remaining question is whether each candidate matches a gene model and a functional ortholog.
