# The annotation desert, revisited

The closed phase made a strong claim and this expansion depends on it being understood correctly, so it deserves a chapter of its own. The claim was never that T. dohrnii lacks genes. It was that the public gene-level infrastructure lacks T. dohrnii. The distinction sounds pedantic until you price it. A researcher who trusts the standard databases sees an empty organism: no gene records, no curated proteins, nothing to download for the eighteen repair and telomerase genes that the longevity literature says to look at first. A researcher who goes to the raw assemblies finds the genes sitting there, unmappable by database search because nobody has deposited the annotation. The same organism is empty and full at once, depending entirely on which door you knock on.

This expansion priced the distinction precisely. On the database side, the audit stands as the closed phase measured it: zero gene-level records for the panel in NCBI nucleotide, gene, and protein for both T. dohrnii and A. aurita, near-zero UniProt coverage, synonym search exhausted. On the genome side, the closed phase's own ledger and this expansion's positive control now quantify the other half: the panel genes map, with strong loci, in the very genome assemblies the databases have nothing to say about. The annotation desert is therefore not a metaphor but a measured gap between two public faces of the same data: the sequence archives, which hold the raw genomes, and the curated gene resources, which have not caught up.

Why does the gap persist? Partly throughput: genome sequencing industrialised faster than genome annotation, and the long tail of non-model organisms is sequenced in draft form far faster than curators and pipelines can annotate. Partly economics: annotation effort follows biomedical funding, and a hydrozoan with no fishery, no pathogen role, and no model-organism community loses out to yet another mammal. And partly structure: automatic annotation propagates from existing annotations, so organisms distant from the annotated core get the worst predictions, which discourages deposition, which keeps them distant. The desert is self-reinforcing.

The consequence for aging and neurology research is the part this project cares most about. Negligibly senescent and rejuvenating species are disproportionately non-model: they are interesting precisely because they are not the standard laboratory animals, which means they are precisely the organisms the curated databases serve worst. Comparative longevity research therefore lives in the annotation desert by necessity, and its methods must be desert-proof: genome-first, calibration-first, threshold-frozen, controls-always. The pipeline the closed phase built and this expansion reuses is one such method, and it is deliberately mundane: BLAST, a fixed bit-score threshold, locus clustering, controls. Nothing in it is clever, which is why it can be trusted where cleverer methods would demand trust in their tuning.

There is also a quiet opportunity in the desert. Because the curated resources are empty, every gene this project maps is, in a small way, a deposition-quality finding: a specific locus, on a specific contig, with specific coordinates, identity, coverage, and evidence, recorded in a public ledger. Aggregated across the three panels, the expansion's ledgers amount to a partial first-pass annotation of the maintenance genome of T. dohrnii, produced as a side effect of asking the research questions. If the field ever builds the proper annotation, these ledgers are scaffolding for it. Until then, they are the difference between knowing the genes are there and merely assuming the 2022 study's genome must contain them.
