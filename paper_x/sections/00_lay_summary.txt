# Lay summary

A tiny jellyfish called Turritopsis dohrnii can do something no other animal can: when it gets old or sick or injured, it turns itself back into its own juvenile form and starts life over. Scientists think this trick must involve the same kinds of genes that control aging in every animal, including us. This project asked a simple question: which of the genes that matter for human aging and for human brain diseases does this jellyfish actually have?

The question turned out to be harder to answer than it should have been, for a surprising reason. When we looked up the jellyfish in the standard public gene databases, we found almost nothing: the databases have no proper gene records for this animal at all. That absence is itself a finding, because it means the world's most interesting longevity organism is nearly invisible to the tools most researchers would use first. So we bypassed the databases and searched the jellyfish's raw genome directly, using carefully checked methods, for two lists of genes: twenty-four genes at the heart of aging and longevity research, and twenty-four genes whose failure causes Alzheimer, Parkinson, ALS, and other brain diseases.

The result is a gene-by-gene map: which of these genes the immortal jellyfish keeps, which it has extra copies of compared with its closest mortal relative, and which it appears to do without. Genes it keeps are ancient maintenance machinery that a rejuvenating animal relies on, and they become candidate entry points for studying rejuvenation in a living animal. Genes it lacks mark the parts of human aging and brain disease that are evolutionary novelties, things a jellyfish cannot teach us about. Both halves matter. We also tested, carefully and with the statistics fixed in advance, whether the genes that connect aging and brain disease cluster together in this genome, and we report what we found whether or not it matched our hopes.

Everything was done with guardrails: the plan was written down before any data were looked at, the methods were borrowed unchanged from earlier validated work, positive controls had to reproduce known results, and fake scrambled genes were run through the same pipeline to prove it does not cry wolf. Where the evidence was weak, we say so. The aim was not to announce a miracle. It was to turn a famous biological marvel into a set of specific, checkable facts that aging researchers and brain researchers can actually use.
