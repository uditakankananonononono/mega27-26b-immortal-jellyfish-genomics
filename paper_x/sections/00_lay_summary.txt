# Lay summary

A tiny jellyfish called Turritopsis dohrnii can do something no other animal can: when it gets old or sick or injured, it turns itself back into its own juvenile form and starts life over. Scientists think this trick must involve the same kinds of genes that control aging in every animal, including us. This project asked a simple question: which of the genes that matter for human aging and for human brain diseases does this jellyfish actually have?

The question turned out to be harder to answer than it should have been, for a surprising reason. When we looked up the jellyfish in the standard public gene databases, we found almost nothing: the main public gene database search returned few usable gene-level records for this assembly, though its genome provider does publish high-confidence gene models. That mismatch makes a raw-assembly search useful, not a claim that annotations do not exist. So we bypassed the databases and searched the jellyfish's raw genome directly, using carefully checked methods, for two lists of genes: twenty-four genes at the heart of aging and longevity research, and twenty-four genes whose failure causes Alzheimer, Parkinson, ALS, and other brain diseases.

The result is a gene-by-gene map: where the assemblies show possible counterparts, copy-number signals, or no detectable strong hit in a fixed search. A matching sequence is a candidate to study, not evidence that the animal uses it to rejuvenate; a missing hit is not proof that the gene is absent. The possible extra ATG5 copy could not be settled with the public long reads. Both halves matter. We also tested, carefully and with the statistics fixed in advance, whether the genes that connect aging and brain disease cluster together in this genome, and we report what we found whether or not it matched our hopes.

Everything was done with guardrails: the plan was written down before any data were looked at, the methods were borrowed unchanged from earlier validated work, positive controls had to reproduce known results, and fake scrambled genes were run through the same pipeline to prove it does not cry wolf. Where the evidence was weak, we say so. The aim was not to announce a miracle. It was to turn a famous biological marvel into a set of specific, checkable facts that aging researchers and brain researchers can actually use.

A later all-protein comparison did not validate a newly expanded family, and a three-stage RNA pilot was too sparse to show a reliable expression change. The new long-read test also did not settle ATG5 copy structure. These limits matter: this report is a transparent map and a set of testable leads, not a discovered cause of aging reversal or a proven new algorithm.
