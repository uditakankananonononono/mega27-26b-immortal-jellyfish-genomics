# What this means for neurology

The neurology chapter of this report rests on a single, defensible premise: neurodegeneration is what maintenance failure looks like in cells that cannot be replaced, and T. dohrnii is an animal whose maintenance never has to fail that way, because its cells, including its neurons, can be sent back and remade. The gene-by-gene map in the results chapter is the evidence; this chapter is about how a neurologically minded reader should use it.

The first use is evolutionary annotation of the human disease-gene list. Every gene in Panel C is famous because of a disease, but each has a normal job, and the depth of that job in evolution tells us which disease processes exploit ancient machinery and which exploit vertebrate novelty. Where the jellyfish genome carries a recognisable homolog of a human disease gene, the protein's core function predates brains, nerve rings, and possibly neurons themselves in their modern form; the human disease is then a corruption of ancient maintenance, and its most basic mechanisms are open to study in simple, regenerating organisms. Where no homolog maps, the disease gene marks a vertebrate addition, and the disease is correspondingly a price paid for vertebrate-specific architecture: complex long-axon neurons, adaptive immunity in the brain's immune compartment, lipid transport tuned to a large cortex. Both halves of that split are knowledge, and before this census neither half existed for this organism.

The second use is the maintenance-bridge test between aging and neurodegeneration. The claim that neurodegeneration is accelerated local aging of irreplaceable cells has been a guiding metaphor in the field for a decade, supported by the convergence of disease pathways on proteostasis, lysosomal clearance, mitophagy, and stress response. The pre-registered H5 test asked the genomic version of that question: do the neurodegeneration genes that map in the rejuvenating jellyfish overlap the longevity genes that map there more than chance? The answer disciplines the metaphor. Where overlap is real, the same ancient maintenance genes are doing double duty in the two problems the user asked to connect, and the jellyfish becomes a legitimate shared model context. Where overlap is absent, the honest conclusion is that the aging-neurodegeneration bridge runs through vertebrate-specific additions rather than the ancient core, and the fields should borrow less freely from each other's model systems.

The third use is regenerative neurology's shopping list. The adult human nervous system cannot do what the jellyfish nervous system does during life cycle reversal: dismantle and rebuild. But the jellyfish does it with genes, and genes have homologs. The genes in Panel C that are present in the jellyfish and diverged from their mortal-cnidarian counterparts are candidate participants in whatever makes jellyfish neural tissue so unusually plastic, and the same logic applies to the stemness and reprogramming factors the 2022 comparative study nominated. None of this says human neurons can be taught the trick; it says that if reprogramming-based neural repair is ever going to work, the parts list is likeliest to be found among genes that a rejuvenating animal actually uses, in a genome this project has now partially indexed.

The fourth use is negative, and valuable because it is negative. A map that marks TREM2-class neuroimmune biology, vertebrate neurotrophin signalling, and APOE-class lipid-risk architecture as evolutionary novelties tells neurodegeneration research where not to look in simple organisms. Cnidarians will never model the microglial biology of Alzheimer disease, and no amount of enthusiasm for the immortal jellyfish changes that. Knowing which third of the disease-gene list requires a vertebrate host is a real deliverable: it redirects effort toward the two-thirds of the list whose foundations are ancient, shared, and suddenly accessible in an animal that rebuilds its nervous system as a matter of routine.

The sober summary for neurology is this. The jellyfish is not a model of any human brain disease, and this report has not made it one. What it now is, after this expansion, is an indexed genome: an organism of known rejuvenative capacity whose complement of neurodegeneration-relevant maintenance genes has been enumerated gene by gene, with calibrated thresholds, controls, and honest negatives. That converts the immortal jellyfish from a source of inspirational metaphors about neural regeneration into a source of specific, checkable statements about which neural-maintenance genes it keeps, which it amplifies, and which it never had.
