# The aging and longevity connection

Why a rejuvenating jellyfish belongs in aging research

Aging research has a standing problem: it is very good at describing what goes wrong in old animals and much worse at explaining why some animals barely age at all. The hallmarks framework, most recently expanded by Lopez-Otin and colleagues in 2023, catalogues the recurring molecular damage and dysregulation of aging: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alteration, loss of proteostasis, disabled macroautophagy, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation, and dysbiosis. The framework is descriptive and mammal-centric. It tells us what a failing maintenance system looks like. It does not tell us what an exceptionally successful one looks like, because the animals it is built on all fail eventually.

Negligibly senescent and rejuvenating organisms are the natural counterweight. Hydra, a freshwater cnidarian relative of T. dohrnii, shows no detectable increase in mortality with age under laboratory conditions, a result established by Martinez in the 1990s and refined by Schaible and colleagues in 2015 with constant mortality and fertility across years of observation. The bowhead whale lives more than two centuries and, as a 2025 Nature study showed, carries quantitatively better DNA double-strand-break repair than shorter-lived mammals. The naked mole rat combines negligible senescence with extraordinary cancer resistance, and comparative genomic work across long-lived mammals repeatedly converges on the same functional neighbourhoods: genome maintenance, telomere biology, proteostasis, nutrient sensing, and tumour suppression. T. dohrnii is the extreme member of this club. It does not merely resist aging; it reverses its effects through a developmentally programmed rejuvenation. If comparative longevity research is serious about learning from the winners, the immortal jellyfish is not an exotic footnote but the limiting case.

The logic of the comparison is what makes Panel B of this expansion necessary. The closed phase mapped the repair and telomerase machinery. But rejuvenation is presumably not only a repair phenomenon. The transcriptomics of life cycle reversal and the Pascual-Torner comparative genomics both point at pluripotency control, redox handling, and intercellular signalling. A rigorous census therefore has to ask, for each of the canonical aging-pathway genes, whether the immortal jellyfish has it at all, whether its copy number or sequence diverges from its mortal relatives, and whether the pattern across genes resembles the pattern in other long-lived species. That is exactly what Panel B was pre-registered to measure.

Panel B: the twenty-four genes and why each is in the longevity canon

The aging and longevity panel was frozen before any data were fetched. Its membership is not arbitrary; each gene is a load-bearing element of the longevity literature, and the panel deliberately spans the major mechanistic categories so that the presence map reads as a profile of maintenance strategies rather than a cherry-picked list.

The insulin and IGF signalling module is the most conserved longevity pathway in the animal kingdom. Reduced signalling through the insulin receptor, IGF1 receptor, and their downstream kinase AKT extends lifespan in worms, flies, and mice, and the same axis implicates human longevity through FOXO3, the forkhead transcription factor whose protective alleles are among the most replicated findings in human longevity genetics, and through the growth hormone receptor GHR, whose loss produces the long-lived, dwarfism-associated Laron syndrome. PTEN, the lipid phosphatase that opposes this pathway, and IGF1 itself complete the module. The nutrient-sensing module continues with MTOR, the kinase at the heart of rapamycin-sensitive growth control and the single most pharmacologically validated aging target, and PRKAA1, the catalytic subunit of AMPK, the energy-depletion sensor whose activation is linked to metformin-like longevity effects.

The sirtuins SIRT1 and SIRT6 represent the NAD-dependent deacetylase family that couples metabolism to chromatin state and DNA repair; SIRT6 in particular has direct lifespan effects in mice and sits at the crossroads of repair and metabolism. The genome-guardian module contains TP53, the master regulator of the DNA-damage response whose calibrated activity balances cancer suppression against stem-cell attrition; CDKN2A, encoding the p16 senescence brake whose expression is one of the most widely used biomarkers of aging; the checkpoint kinases ATM and ATR; and the RecQ helicases BLM and WRN, whose loss produces the human progeroid Bloom and Werner syndromes. These last two are the strongest human genetic evidence that helicase-driven genome maintenance is a rate-limiting aging system: break the helicase, get premature aging.

The proteostasis and quality-control module contains NFE2L2, encoding the NRF2 transcription factor that orchestrates the oxidative-stress response and is recurrently implicated in longevity; HSF1, the heat-shock factor that maintains the folding environment and declines with age; the autophagy core genes ATG5 and ATG7, whose loss shortens lifespan and whose induction extends it in multiple models; BECN1, the beclin that initiates autophagosome formation; and TFEB, the master transcriptional regulator of lysosomal biogenesis, whose overexpression clears aggregates and extends healthspan in model systems. Finally, APOE sits deliberately on the boundary of Panels B and C: its alleles are the strongest common genetic influence on both human longevity and Alzheimer disease risk, making it the single clearest molecular bridge between the two fields the user asked to connect.

What the jellyfish can and cannot tell us about these genes

Three outcomes are possible for each Panel B gene, and all three are informative. A gene may be present and conserved in T. dohrnii, which identifies it as ancient metazoan maintenance machinery and makes its handling in a rejuvenating animal a legitimate mechanistic question: does the immortal jellyfish use the same gene differently? A gene may be present but expanded or diverged relative to T. rubra and A. aurita, which is the signature the Pascual-Torner study reported for repair genes and which, if it recurs in the longevity panel, nominates specific maintenance systems as candidate rejuvenation adaptations. Or a gene may be absent from the jellyfish genome within the calibrated limits of the method, which is equally meaningful: it would show that a recognisable version of that maintenance pathway is dispensable for biological immortality, and therefore that the pathway's role in human aging is a derived feature of our lineage rather than a universal constraint.

The copy-number contrast deserves a special caveat that this report will keep visible. The immortal-versus-mortal comparison here runs across four genomes: two T. dohrnii assemblies, one T. rubra, one A. aurita. That is a powerful natural design for generating hypotheses and a weak one for proving adaptation, because every contrast is confounded with assembly quality, annotation state, and the deep phylogenetic distance between the genera. The pre-registered H3 and H4 tests are therefore written as screens, and their positive results are nominations, not discoveries. The report holds to that discipline even where the results are pleasing.

Connection to the wider longevity-genomics record

Placed against the mammalian longevity literature, the jellyfish census plays a distinctive role. Mammalian studies can compare long-lived and short-lived species that are all, ultimately, aging species; their contrasts are quantitative. The Turritopsis comparison offers a qualitative one: mortality endpoint versus no obligatory mortality endpoint, inside the same genus. If a maintenance gene is expanded in the immortal species relative to its mortal congener, that is a cleaner signal than most cross-order mammalian comparisons can provide, because T. dohrnii and T. rubra share ecology, body plan, and most of their genome. The cost is statistical power: one genus, one contrast. The benefit is specificity. This is the trade the expansion makes openly, and it is why every positive contrast is paired with an explicit statement of what would be needed to confirm it: orthogonal evidence from independent assemblies, transcriptomics across the reversal timecourse, and eventually functional perturbation, which is now feasible in cnidarians by CRISPR.

The deliverable for aging research, then, is a prioritised, falsifiable map: which longevity genes the immortal jellyfish keeps, which it amplifies, and which it does without. Each row of that map is a small, testable claim about the necessary and dispensable parts of the maintenance toolkit, generated by an animal that already solved the problem the field is trying to solve.
