# Background: what the closed phase established, and where the boundary lay

The organism and its trick

Turritopsis dohrnii is a hydrozoan in the family Oceaniidae, originally described from the Mediterranean and now known to be widely distributed in warm and temperate seas. Like most hydrozoans it has a biphasic life cycle: a benthic, colonial polyp stage buds off free-swimming medusae, which grow, reproduce sexually, and in every other known medusozoan species, die. The defining observation for T. dohrnii, built up through the 1990s and consolidated in the literature since, is that its medusae respond to stress, starvation, or senescence by reverting to the polyp stage. The medusa settles, its tissues reorganise, and a stolon and then a polyp emerge. The transformation involves transdifferentiation and, in some tissues, proliferation of a reactivated stem-like cell population. The polyp then grows and buds new medusae, completing a loop that has no obligatory endpoint.

Two features of this biology matter for everything that follows. The first is that rejuvenation is whole-body and includes the nervous system. The medusa has a nerve ring, ocelli, and differentiated neural cell types; the polyp has a simpler but genuine nerve net. During reversal the medusa's neural structures are dismantled and a new nervous system is built. Whatever molecular machinery permits that degree of neural plasticity is operating in a living, intact, evolutionarily successful animal, not in a dish. The second feature is that reversal is not immortality in the literal sense: individual animals can still be eaten, infected, or fatally damaged, and the efficiency of reversal declines with repeated stress in laboratory settings. The accurate statement is that T. dohrnii lacks an obligatory senescent endpoint for the medusa stage, and that this is already strange enough to demand a genomic explanation.

The reference point: comparative genomics of mortal and immortal cnidarians

The field's anchor is the 2022 PNAS study by Pascual-Torner and colleagues, which sequenced and compared the genomes of T. dohrnii and its non-reverting congener T. rubra, and profiled the transcriptome of the life cycle reversal process itself. The headline findings were variants and expansions of genes associated with replication, DNA repair, telomere maintenance, redox environment, stem-cell population maintenance, and intercellular communication in the immortal species, together with silencing of polycomb repressive complex 2 targets and activation of pluripotency targets during reversal. The interpretation offered was that rejuvenation capacity rests on an unusually well-equipped genome maintenance and reprogramming toolkit.

That paper is both the motivation and the caution for the present project. The motivation is obvious: if repair, telomere, and stemness genes are expanded or diverged in the immortal species, a systematic gene-by-gene census of those systems is directly informative. The caution is subtler. Expansions and variants reported from a two-species comparison are hypotheses about mechanism, not proofs of it, and they depend entirely on the quality of the underlying annotation. If the annotation is incomplete or inaccessible, the comparison cannot be reproduced, and a claim that cannot be reproduced cannot be trusted or extended. That is exactly where the closed phase of this project found the public resources to be.

The annotation desert, measured

The closed phase of item 26b began with a simple plan: fetch the eighteen genes of a curated DNA-repair and telomerase panel for T. dohrnii and the moon jelly Aurelia aurita from the standard public databases, and compare them against human and Hydra vulgaris references. The plan failed in an instructive way. For T. dohrnii, systematic queries of the NCBI nucleotide, gene, and protein databases, with synonym expansion including the older name Turritopsis nutricula, returned zero gene-level records for every gene in the panel. UniProt held only a handful of T. dohrnii entries, none in the panel. The same pattern held for A. aurita. The closed-phase route then used raw genome assemblies: a contig-level assembly (GCA_027922465.2) and the scaffold-level assembly associated with the Oviedo group (GCA_025167195.1), plus the A. aurita assembly GCA_004194415.1. This statement is restricted to the queried database fields: Kyushu separately published 23,314 high-confidence predicted genes and expression data for its T. dohrnii assembly (Hasegawa et al. 2023).

The closed-phase report called the selected query miss an annotation desert. In this case it was a retrieval gap in chosen databases, not proof of scarce public predictions for the organism. The practical consequence of mistaking this particular query miss for a complete resource census would be a false absence claim. Absence of annotation is not absence of genes. The closed phase proved the distinction by an independent route: it bypassed the databases, took the raw genomes, and mapped the genes directly.

The calibrated pipeline of the closed phase

The mapping pipeline that the closed phase validated, and that this expansion reuses unchanged, is deliberately simple and auditable. Curated query proteins, human RefSeq references plus cnidarian homologs from annotated species (Clytia hemisphaerica, Hydra vulgaris, Acropora, Nematostella), are searched against each genome with translated BLAST (tBLASTn 2.17.0, e-value threshold 1e-5). Hits are clustered into candidate loci on single contigs within a fixed span, each locus accumulates a total bit score across its high-scoring segment pairs, and a locus is called strong when its total bit score reaches a frozen threshold of one hundred. Strong loci are extracted with flanks for downstream analysis. The thresholds were fixed before the closed phase's outcome analyses and are reused here verbatim; the expansion changes the queries and adds one comparator genome, nothing else.

On this pipeline the closed phase established that the repair and telomerase panel is physically present in the T. dohrnii genome. The locus ledger records, for example, a high-scoring FEN1 locus with nearly ninety percent query coverage and strong matches for MLH1 and other panel genes, in both T. dohrnii assemblies. A dedicated telomerase deep-check found the catalytic TERT locus, with its characteristic reverse-transcriptase and telomerase RNA-binding domains, in all three scanned genomes, including the mortal A. aurita, a result that immediately complicates any naive story in which telomerase presence alone explains immortality. A separate arm showed that the telomerase RNA component TERC, which is not a protein and therefore invisible to translated search, could not be detected by short-sequence alignment in the T. dohrnii assemblies, an honest negative that delimits what the method can and cannot see. Machine-learning arms (a convolutional classifier and a graph neural network over sequence composition) were trained and evaluated with an honest-negative register; the graph model did not beat baseline, and that was reported as such.

Why the expansion starts from genomes, not databases

The combined lesson of the closed phase is the methodological premise of this expansion: for organisms in the annotation desert, direct genome mapping is a useful independent check, not the only possible route. Every claim in the chapters that follow is therefore built on direct genome mapping with calibrated thresholds, positive controls that reproduce the closed phase's calls, and negative controls built from shuffled query sequences. The original mapping used databases to source query proteins from reference species, but later arms also inspected provider gene models and their RNA evidence. Where the expansion extends beyond the closed phase, it does so by adding pre-registered panels and a new comparator genome, not by relaxing the evidentiary standard. The boundary the closed phase found, between what is annotated and what is present, is the boundary this expansion was built to cross.
