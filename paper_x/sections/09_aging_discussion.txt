# What this means for aging and longevity research

The point of mapping the longevity canon onto the genome of a rejuvenating animal is not to find the gene of immortality. There is no such gene, and this study did not look for one. The point is sharper: the hallmarks framework and the GenAge canon were built almost entirely from animals that age. Running that canon across an animal that does not obligatorily age is a direct test of which of its elements are universal maintenance machinery and which are features of aging-prone lineages. The results chapters give the gene-by-gene account; this chapter draws the synthesis.

The first synthesis is about the architecture of the panel itself. The aging canon is not a flat list but a layered system: sensing layers that read nutrients and damage, control layers that decide between growth and maintenance, and execution layers that repair DNA, clear damaged proteins and organelles, and police cell identity. The mapping pattern across those layers in the T. dohrnii genome, set against the mortal cnidarians, is the most compact description this project can offer of what the immortal jellyfish's longevity toolkit shares with ours and where it parts company. The execution layers are the ancient part. Repair kinases, helicases, autophagy enzymes, proteostasis factors, and their regulators belong to the oldest stratum of eukaryotic, and often metazoan, machinery, and the census reflects that depth. The derived part is the regulatory superstructure that vertebrates, and especially mammals, layered on top: the growth-hormone axis, the p16 senescence brake, the lipid-transport risk architecture of APOE. Where the jellyfish genome does not carry those genes, the absence is not a gap in the jellyfish; it is a marker of how much of human aging biology is recently built scaffolding around an ancient core.

The second synthesis concerns the immortal-versus-mortal Turritopsis contrast. This is the comparison the whole field wants, because T. dohrnii and T. rubra share a genus, a body plan, and most of a genome, yet one reverts and the other does not. The pre-registered copy-contrast test was designed to nominate candidate expansion genes in the longevity panel the way the 2022 comparative-genomics study nominated them in the repair panel. The honest reading of any such contrast in a two-genome comparison, with one assembly contig-level and the contrast measured against two mortal species at different phylogenetic distances, is that every surviving signal is a nomination for mechanistic follow-up rather than a discovered adaptation. That restraint is not a hedge; it is the difference between a screen and a claim, and it is what makes the nominations worth following up.

The third synthesis is about what the jellyfish adds to the comparative-longevity canon beyond the bowhead whales, naked mole rats, bats, and beavers that currently anchor it. Those mammals refine the same mammalian toolkit toward longer life; their lessons are dosage lessons. The jellyfish offers a different kind of lesson, a design lesson: an organism whose maintenance machinery never has to choose senescence because rejuvenation is always an option. In the language of the hallmarks, T. dohrnii does not merely slow stem-cell exhaustion; it periodically resets the stem-cell compartment wholesale. The panel results locate the shared machinery on which such a reset must run, and thereby convert a biological marvel into a list of molecular entry points, each one a gene that aging research already cares about, now with an annotated status in the only animal that restarts its life.

The fourth synthesis is the practical one, for the researchers the user asked this work to speak to. For the hallmarks community, the jellyfish census is an existence proof that the hallmarks are separable: an animal can hold the core maintenance hallmarks indefinitely while the vertebrate regulatory additions came and went in evolution. For the GenAge and comparative-genomics community, it adds the limiting-case species that makes the long-lived-mammal contrasts interpretable, because a toolkit shared with T. dohrnii is a toolkit that plausibly predates aging as we know it. For the intervention community, the mapped set is a filter: a longevity target with a jellyfish counterpart is a target whose basic biology can be studied in a fast, cheap, regenerating whole animal before it is pursued in mammals. And for everyone, the annotation-desert finding of the closed phase, now resolved into an actual gene census, is the reminder that the most interesting genomes are the ones the databases serve worst, and that going to the genome directly is often the only honest option.
