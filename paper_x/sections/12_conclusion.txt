# Conclusion and next steps

This expansion set out to do one thing: take the closed phase's calibrated jellyfish genomics and aim it at the two fields the user named, aging and longevity research and neurology. It did that by freezing two new gene panels, adding the chromosome-level mortal-congener genome, reusing the validated mapping pipeline without touching its thresholds, gating everything on positive and negative controls, and pre-registering six hypotheses with their corrections before any outcome data existed.

The deliverables are durable regardless of which way individual hypotheses fell. There is now a gene-by-gene census of the aging and longevity canon in the genome of the only metazoan that repeatedly rejuvenates, and a matching census of the human neurodegeneration gene set, both built on direct genome mapping with recorded checksums, manifests, and ledgers rather than on databases that this organism barely exists in. There is a tested statement about the copy-number contrast between the immortal species and its mortal relatives. There is a tested statement about whether the aging and neurology panels share their mappable core in this genome. And there is a negative register that says plainly what did not survive correction, because a screen that cannot fail is not a screen.

The next steps write themselves from the results, and they split by evidence class. For every gene where both T. dohrnii assemblies agree and controls are clean, the follow-up is transcriptomic: does the gene's expression move during life cycle reversal, in the published reversal timecourse and in new data? For every copy-number contrast that survived correction, the follow-up is structural: build the locus properly with long-read data, because copy claims from draft assemblies are provisional by nature. For every absence that matters, the follow-up is sensitivity: longer queries, profile methods, and eventually the annotation effort this organism has always deserved. And for the two fields this report connects, the follow-up is collaborative: the aging community now has a checklist for the limiting-case species, and the neurology community has an evolutionary annotation of its disease-gene list that did not exist before this week.

The immortal jellyfish does not owe anyone a cure. What it owes science, and what this project has collected, is a straight answer to a straight question: what does the genome of the animal that restarts its life actually contain of the machinery that human aging and human neurodegeneration run on? The answer is now on record, gene by gene, with its uncertainties printed next to it.
