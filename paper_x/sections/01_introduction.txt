# Introduction: the jellyfish that restarts its life, and what it owes to aging research and neurology

Most animals are born, mature, reproduce, and age in one direction. Turritopsis dohrnii, a hydrozoan jellyfish barely five millimetres across, is the only known metazoan that can run this sequence backwards. When a medusa of this species is starved, injured, or simply old, it does not have to die. It sinks to the seabed, retracts its tentacles, loses the differentiated medusa form, and rebuilds a polyp, the juvenile stage of the life cycle, from its existing cells. That polyp can bud off new medusae that are genetic copies of the original. In principle the loop can repeat indefinitely, which is why the popular press named it the immortal jellyfish and why aging researchers have watched it with a mixture of envy and suspicion ever since the life cycle reversal was first described in detail.

The biological interest is not the immortality headline. It is the mechanism. Life cycle reversal in T. dohrnii is a whole-organism act of cellular rejuvenation. A differentiated adult cell population is induced to abandon its specialised identity and reacquire a younger developmental state, after which the organism rebuilds itself. In the vocabulary of modern biomedicine this is in vivo reprogramming without an experimenter, and it sits conceptually next to the two most discussed technologies in aging research: partial reprogramming by forced expression of pluripotency factors, and the still poorly understood ability of some regeneration-competent animals to rebuild damaged or missing tissue without scarring or loss of pattern. If a five-millimetre cnidarian can reboot its entire body repeatedly, then rejuvenation is not forbidden by the laws of metazoan biology; it is a trait that evolution has produced at least once, and traits that evolution has produced can, in principle, be studied, mechanistically decomposed, and eventually imitated.

That is the starting point of this project. This expansion connects jellyfish genomics to aging and longevity research and to neurology. Those connections are not decorative. The first is direct: rejuvenation is the inverse of aging, so the genome of a rejuvenating animal is a natural experiment in the genetics of age resistance, and the genes it shares with human longevity pathways are candidate levers for healthy lifespan extension. The second is less obvious but equally strong. The adult human brain is the organ least able to replace its cells; neurodegenerative disease is, in large part, the slow failure of maintenance biology in long-lived post-mitotic neurons. A creature whose nervous system dissolves and reforms during rejuvenation is a creature whose neurons tolerate a degree of plasticity that human neurons never experience. Understanding what makes that tolerance possible speaks directly to the two great unmet needs of neurology: stopping neurodegeneration, and inducing regeneration where degeneration has already occurred.

This document is the expansion report of MEGA27 item 26b. The closed phase of the project established two things. First, an audit of public databases showed that the standard gene-level resources (NCBI nucleotide, gene, and protein databases, and UniProt) contain essentially no curated gene records for T. dohrnii: for an eighteen-gene DNA-repair and telomerase panel, the count of gene-level records was zero, and only raw genome assemblies existed. That annotation desert is itself a result about the state of public biology. Second, the closed phase built a working translated-homology mapping pipeline (tBLASTn with frozen thresholds, locus clustering, and locus extraction) and demonstrated that the panel genes are in fact physically present and mappable in the two available T. dohrnii genome assemblies, with a curated locus ledger as the deliverable. The closed phase therefore ended with a calibrated boundary: the genes exist in the genome but are invisible to the databases that most computational workflows would consult first.

The expansion takes the next three steps. It widens the gene panel from DNA repair and telomere maintenance to two new, pre-registered panels: twenty-four aging and longevity genes chosen from the core of the hallmarks-of-aging and GenAge literature (Panel B), and twenty-four neurology and neurodegeneration genes comprising the major monogenic disease genes and neurotrophic factors (Panel C). It adds a new comparator genome, the chromosome-level assembly of Turritopsis rubra, the non-reverting congener that anchors the natural contrast between a rejuvenating and a non-rejuvenating species. And it applies the frozen, already-validated mapping pipeline, unchanged, with positive and negative controls, so that every presence or absence claim in the new panels inherits the calibration of the closed phase rather than resting on fresh, untuned judgement.

Everything in this expansion is pre-registered. The analysis plan, the panel gene lists, the hypotheses H1 through H6, the statistical tests, the multiple-testing correction, and the honest-negative register were committed to the repository before any outcome data were fetched. If the aging panel fails to map, that is reported. If the neurology panel shows no enrichment, that is reported. If the copy-number contrast between the immortal and the mortal Turritopsis species evaporates under correction, that is reported. The purpose of the project is not to manufacture a discovery; it is to find out what is actually in the genome of the animal that restarts its life, and to route whatever is found, positive or negative, into the two fields that the user asked to see connected: the science of aging and longevity, and the science of the degenerating and regenerating brain.

A note on scope and honesty is warranted here, because the word immortality invites overclaiming. Nothing in this work shows that any human disease gene has a functional ortholog in the jellyfish, let alone that the jellyfish uses it the way humans do. Nothing here demonstrates that a jellyfish longevity gene would extend human healthspan. What this work does establish, gene by gene and genome by genome, is the substrate: which parts of the human aging and neurological-disease machinery have ancient, mappable counterparts in a rejuvenating cnidarian, which parts are absent, and where the copy-number and sequence signals differ between the immortal species and its mortal relatives. Mechanistic claims belong to the laboratory. This is a computational atlas, and it is written to be useful as one.

The structure of the report follows the logic of the work. Chapter 2 summarises the closed phase and the state of public resources for this organism. Chapter 3 develops the aging and longevity connection in depth, from the hallmarks framework to the comparative genomics of long-lived species. Chapter 4 develops the neurology connection, from the genetics of neurodegeneration to the peculiar biology of the cnidarian nervous system. Chapter 5 describes the expansion methods, including the controls and the statistical protocol. Chapters 6 and 7 report the mapping results for the aging and neurology panels gene by gene. Chapter 8 reports the pre-registered hypothesis tests, including the ones that failed. Chapter 9 discusses what the results mean for aging and longevity research, and Chapter 10 does the same for neurology. Chapter 11 collects the honest negatives and the limitations, and Chapter 12 concludes with the most defensible next experiments.

The framing of this project follows an archetype that has succeeded at the top level of student science fairs: identify a biomarker where none exists, verify it through orthogonal lines of evidence, and state a concrete translational path. The template case is Natasha Kulviwat's Regeneron ISEF 2023 project, which won the Gordon E. Moore Award for nominating claudin-5 as a novel biomarker of suicide pathogenesis: human postmortem brain tissue stratified by cause of death supplied the primary evidence, ELISA, immunolocalization, and public RNA-sequencing differential-expression and pathway analyses supplied orthogonal verification, and molecular docking of existing drugs against the biomarker supplied the translational arm (Society for Science project 23691). This project applies the same structure at the genomic level. The exploratory candidates are named loci whose detection or apparent copy-number pattern can be tested across assemblies and relatives; none is a validated biomarker of rejuvenation. The orthogonal verification arms are assembly replication, negative controls, annotated-genome calibration, and panel-versus-panel contrasts. The translational perspective is a literature connection to human aging and neurodegeneration biology; it neither establishes conserved function nor verifies a therapeutic target in this species. What the archetype demands, and what the results chapters therefore deliver, is a named top candidate with a falsifiable validation plan rather than statistics alone.
